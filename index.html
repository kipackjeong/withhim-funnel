The part nobody says out loud

Something else is already there.

The alarm goes off and your hand is already on the phone, because the phone is the alarm. You swipe it away, and then — since it's already unlocked and already in your hand — you check one thing. Then a second thing. Then it's 6:52 and you need to be in the shower. Before you have said a word to anyone, before you have said a word to God, you have read forty things written by strangers.

The evening has the same shape from the other end. A ten-minute scroll doesn't feel like ten minutes; it feels like ninety seconds, right up until you check the clock. Then the light goes off, and the last voice in your head is the feed's.

The Bible app is on that phone too. It has been for two years. It's four taps away, and it has never once been the thing you opened at 6:41, because at 6:41 you aren't making a decision. You're following a groove.

None of that is a motivation problem. You are not short on conviction, and you do not need persuading that Scripture is worth your time — you settled that years ago and you have not changed your mind. The feed simply arrives first. It gets to you at 6:40 and again at 11pm, every day, and it doesn't need you to be motivated. It only needs to be earlier.

So the fix isn't to want it more. The fix is to be there first.

With Him — the Bible app that makes sure you actually read it.

A complete, offline Bible, wrapped around two mechanics no other Bible app has: an alarm that ends when you read a verse aloud, and a shield that interrupts a long scroll with the same thirty seconds.

How it works

Two moments where the phone always wins. Those are the only two we take.

Every other Bible app is a library. It sits on the home screen and waits to be opened, which means it gets opened by people who were already going to open it. Fine for a library. Hopeless against a feed.

This one doesn't wait. It shows up at exactly two moments and nowhere else — the minute you wake, and the minute the scroll has gone on too long.

Moment one — the alarm

You set it the way you'd set any alarm: time, days, sound. It fires with the ringer switch off and through a Focus mode, it survives a restart, and there's no snooze — snooze is the bypass wearing a friendlier name.

When you stop it, the phone opens straight onto a verse and the alarm sound keeps playing there. You read the verse out loud. The words light up one by one as they're recognised. When you reach the end, the sound stops and your day starts — this time from the other side.

Walk away without reading and the alarm returns in two minutes. No passage runs longer than sixty spoken words, a ceiling written into the product, which keeps every morning under thirty seconds.

Moment two — the scroll

You choose which apps to shield. After about ten minutes of near-continuous scrolling in one of them, the app is covered and the same verse is waiting behind a single button.

Read it out loud and you go straight back, with a fresh block of time — fifteen minutes by default, and you set the number. This isn't a wall. It's the thirty seconds you'd have given the morning, arriving at the hour you'd otherwise have lost.

"About ten minutes" is deliberately vague, because iOS measures app usage in a way that makes precision dishonest. And turning the shield off takes three taps, always — no maze, no confirm-you-really-mean-it screen, no penalty for switching it off at 9pm on a Friday.

Why out loud, and not a tap

Because a tap proves nothing. You can clear a verse-of-the-day notification with your eyes shut, and most mornings that's precisely what happens. Nothing in a swipe is evidence that a single word arrived.

Speaking is different in a way you feel the first time you try it. You can't say a sentence out loud while thinking about something else — the words have to go through you to come out of you. It's slower than reading and more awkward than reading, and both of those are the point.

You don't have to take that on faith. There's a verse on the next page and a microphone in your device.

The limit of all this

iOS lets you force-quit any app on your phone, this one included. Nobody can build an alarm you cannot escape, and anyone selling you one is telling you something you'll disprove on day two.

So here's the actual claim. It doesn't make the wrong choice impossible. It makes the wrong choice more expensive than thirty seconds of reading — which, on most mornings, turns out to be enough. If that's a weaker promise than you expected, it is. It's also the true one.

The fair question

"So Scripture is a toll gate now?"

The gate is a doorbell, not a debt. It has no opinion on whether you're a good Christian, it isn't keeping a ledger, and reading a verse into your phone at 6:40 earns you nothing — from God or from the app. All it does is put the words in front of you at the one moment you'd have missed them, then get out of the way.

In the software that means there is no failure state. It will never tell you that you failed, never scold you, never send a notification about a day you missed. If you can't finish, there's a permanent alternative with no penalty and no asterisk in your stats. If you switch everything off for a month, nothing chases you. And if it ever starts to feel like law rather than invitation, delete it — three taps, no exit survey. An app that made that hard would be exactly the thing you're worried it might be.

The honest framing is that this is a commitment device, the same family as putting your running shoes by the door. Nobody is coercing you. You're making a thing you already decided to do slightly harder to skip than it was yesterday.

What you actually read

Not a random verse a day. A sequence that goes somewhere.

Verse-of-the-day, in most apps, is a shuffle. Judges on Tuesday, Ephesians on Wednesday, half a line of Proverbs on Thursday. Each is fine alone and none of them accumulate into anything, which is part of why they're so easy to swipe away.

Here the daily verse comes from a Thread — an ordered run of passages on one theme, arranged deliberately, and read in order.

Anxiety · 4 of 14

A Thread on anxiety doesn't just gather every calming verse in the canon. It names the condition honestly, moves through the character of God as the ground of peace, and ends by sending you back out to do something. Where you are in that arc sits on the home screen: Anxiety · 4 of 14. You always know what you read yesterday and roughly where this is going.

Twelve Threads at launch, more than two hundred entries between them — anxiety, identity, grief, purpose, temptation, gratitude, forgiveness, waiting, courage, provision, rest, and the life of Christ.

The alarm and the shield both pull from your current Thread, so the verse that stops your alarm and the verse that interrupts your scroll belong to the same sequence. Reading aloud moves the story forward rather than sitting outside it.

They're written by hand, one at a time, with review — a real chunk of the work between now and launch, and the part waitlist members get a vote on.

Your voice

There are no recordings, because nothing is ever recorded.

This is the question the microphone earns, and the answer is structural rather than a promise on a policy page.

  • Speech is processed on your device. Recognition runs on the iPhone itself. Verify it the way the developer does: put the phone in airplane mode and the alarm still works.
  • Audio is never written to disk and never transmitted. What survives a reading is a pass or fail and a similarity score. The sound of your voice does not exist a second after you've spoken.
  • No account, no server, no analytics vendor. Nothing to log into, nothing to breach, and nowhere for a record of what you read to go.
  • Highlights and notes sync through your own iCloud, on your own quota, between your own devices. Nobody else can see them.

One honest exception, and it applies to the demo rather than the app. The web demo has to use your browser's built-in speech recognition, and in some browsers — Safari among them — that routes a few seconds of audio to the browser maker's servers. That's a limitation of the web, not a choice, and it's flagged again directly under the microphone button. The shipped iPhone app does not work that way and never will.

Questions

The things people ask first.

I share a room. I can't read out loud at 6am.

The most common practical blocker, and it has a real answer rather than a workaround. Trace-to-read is a permanent silent alternative — you trace the words with your finger instead of speaking them. Set it up in onboarding and leave it on forever. It isn't a punishment path, it doesn't count as a bypass in your own stats, and you can try it on the demo page in the next thirty seconds.

What if it doesn't recognise how I say things?

The single biggest technical risk in the product, and it's being treated that way. Verification is coverage-based rather than exact, so it tolerates filler words, false starts, self-corrections, and a hoarse 6am voice. A failed attempt keeps the progress you made. After three attempts or ninety seconds, an "I can't speak right now" option appears with a silent way through. Thresholds are tuned against real recordings across a range of accents before launch, not after.

Is there a snooze button?

No, deliberately — snooze is the bypass wearing a friendly hat. The secondary control offers a shorter verse instead, which keeps the mechanic intact on the mornings you have nothing in the tank.

Do I really need another subscription?

Maybe not. Genuinely — if the free reader is all you want, take it and never pay a cent. If you want the habit features, the prices are published above rather than hidden until checkout, and most people reading this have already paid for a commitment device or two.

It'll just be another app I abandon.

Probably a fair prediction, and we won't argue you out of it. Most habit apps fail for one reason: they add another thing to remember. This adds nothing. It attaches to an alarm you were already setting and a scroll you were already starting — a different failure mode from the ones you've lived through, not a guarantee.

And the stakes right now are roughly zero. Joining costs an email. If the launch note lands and you've stopped caring, ignore it.

Can I set this up for my teenager, or my spouse?

No, and it's better to say so now than after you've paid. Version one uses individual, self-imposed authorisation — you set it for yourself and you can turn it off yourself in three taps. There's no parent-side control and no way to lock someone else in. Recommend it to someone if you like; you just can't impose it.

Which translation? Is Android coming?

The Berean Standard Bible is bundled at launch — modern, readable, public domain, and it reads well out loud, which matters more here than in a normal Bible app. WEB and ASV are planned as free alternates. ESV and NIV need negotiated licences a solo developer can't get before proving the product works.

iPhone only at launch. No Android, no iPad layout, no web app. If you're on Android there's honestly nothing worth putting you on a list for yet.

When does it launch, and who's building it?

No date, because any date given now would be a guess dressed as a commitment. The reader comes first, then the alarm, then the shield — and the shield waits on Apple approving an entitlement, on a timeline nobody outside Apple controls. Waitlist members get TestFlight builds in waves, starting small, and hear about slips as they happen.

One developer, working alone. Not a pastor, not a ministry, not a team. This exists because his own mornings looked exactly like the ones at the top of this page. There's no authority behind it beyond someone who had the problem and can write software.
