The part nobody says out loud

Something else is already there.

The alarm is the phone, so your hand is already on it. You swipe, and because it's unlocked and already in your hand, you check one thing. Then it's 6:52. The evening runs the same way from the other end, and the last voice in your head is the feed's.

The Bible app is on that phone too. It's four taps away and has been for two years.

That isn't a motivation problem. You settled whether Scripture is worth your time years ago. The feed simply arrives first — and it doesn't need you motivated, only earlier.

So the fix isn't to want it more. The fix is to be there first.

With Him — the Bible app that makes sure you actually read it.

A complete, offline Bible, wrapped around two mechanics no other Bible app has: an alarm that ends when you read a verse aloud, and a shield that interrupts a long scroll with the same thirty seconds.

How it works

Two moments where the phone always wins. Those are the only two we take.

Every other Bible app waits on the home screen to be opened, which means it gets opened by people who were already going to open it. This one doesn't wait. It shows up at exactly two moments and nowhere else — the minute you wake, and the minute the scroll has gone on too long.

Moment one — the alarm

Set it like any alarm. When you stop it, the phone opens onto a verse and the sound keeps playing. You read it out loud, the words light up as they're recognised, and at the end the sound stops.

Walk away without reading and it returns in two minutes. No passage runs past sixty spoken words, so no morning runs past thirty seconds. There's no snooze — snooze is the bypass wearing a friendlier name.

Moment two — the scroll

You choose which apps to shield. After about ten minutes of near-continuous scrolling in one of them, the app is covered and the same verse waits behind a single button.

Read it out loud and you go straight back with a fresh block of time. This isn't a wall — it's the thirty seconds you'd have given the morning, arriving at the hour you'd otherwise have lost. Switching the shield off takes three taps, always.

Why out loud, and not a tap

You can clear a verse-of-the-day notification with your eyes shut. Speaking is different in a way you feel the first time: you can't say a sentence out loud while thinking about something else. It's slower and more awkward than reading, and both are the point.

You don't have to take that on faith. There's a verse on the next page and a microphone in your device.

The limit of all this

iOS lets you force-quit any app, this one included. Nobody can build an alarm you cannot escape. So the actual claim is smaller: it doesn't make the wrong choice impossible, it makes the wrong choice more expensive than thirty seconds of reading — which, most mornings, turns out to be enough. A weaker promise than you expected, and the true one.

The fair question

"So Scripture is a toll gate now?"

The gate is a doorbell, not a debt. Reading a verse into your phone at 6:40 earns you nothing — from God or from the app. It puts the words in front of you at the one moment you'd have missed them, then gets out of the way.

So there is no failure state. It never tells you that you failed, never scolds, never sends a notification about a day you missed. Miss a month and nothing chases you. It's a commitment device, the same family as putting your running shoes by the door — a thing you already decided to do, made slightly harder to skip.

What you actually read

Not a random verse a day. A sequence that goes somewhere.

Verse-of-the-day, in most apps, is a shuffle: Judges on Tuesday, Ephesians on Wednesday. Each is fine alone and none of them accumulate, which is part of why they're so easy to swipe away.

Here the daily verse comes from a Thread — an ordered run of passages on one theme, read in order.

Anxiety · 4 of 14

A Thread on anxiety names the condition, moves through the character of God as the ground of peace, and ends by sending you back out. Where you are in that arc sits on the home screen: Anxiety · 4 of 14.

The alarm and the shield both pull from your current Thread, so reading aloud moves the sequence forward rather than sitting outside it. Twelve Threads planned for launch — anxiety, identity, grief, purpose, temptation, gratitude, forgiveness, waiting, courage, provision, rest, and the life of Christ. They're written by hand, and waitlist members get a vote on the order.

Your voice

There are no recordings, because nothing is ever recorded.

This is the question the microphone earns, and the answer is structural rather than a promise on a policy page.

  • Speech is processed on your device. Verify it the way the developer does: airplane mode, and the alarm still works.
  • Audio is never written to disk and never transmitted. What survives a reading is a pass or fail. The sound of your voice doesn't exist a second after you've spoken.
  • No account, no server, no analytics vendor. Nothing to breach, and nowhere for a record of what you read to go.
  • Highlights and notes sync through your own iCloud, on your own quota. Nobody else can see them.

One exception, and it's the demo rather than the app: the web demo uses your browser's speech recognition, which in Safari routes a few seconds of audio to Apple. A limitation of the web, flagged again under the microphone button. The iPhone app never works that way.

Questions

The things people ask first.

I share a room. I can't read out loud at 6am.

Trace-to-read is a permanent silent alternative — you trace the words with your finger instead of speaking them. Set it in onboarding and leave it on forever. Not a punishment path, and not counted as a bypass. You can try it on the demo page right now.

What if it doesn't recognise how I say things?

Verification is coverage-based rather than exact, so it tolerates filler words, false starts, and a hoarse 6am voice, and a failed attempt keeps the progress you made. After three attempts an "I can't speak right now" option appears with a silent way through.

Is there a snooze button?

No, deliberately — snooze is the bypass wearing a friendly hat. The secondary control offers a shorter verse instead, which keeps the mechanic intact on the mornings you have nothing in the tank.

Do I really need another subscription?

Maybe not. Genuinely — if the free reader is all you want, take it and never pay a cent. If you want the habit features, the prices are published above rather than hidden until checkout, and most people reading this have already paid for a commitment device or two.

It'll just be another app I abandon.

Probably a fair prediction. Most habit apps fail for one reason — they add another thing to remember. This attaches to an alarm you were already setting and a scroll you were already starting. A different failure mode, not a guarantee. And joining costs an email; if the launch note lands and you've stopped caring, ignore it.

Can I set this up for my teenager, or my spouse?

No. Version one is self-imposed only — you set it for yourself and can turn it off yourself in three taps. There's no parent-side control and no way to lock someone else in. Recommend it if you like; you can't impose it.

Which translation? Is Android coming?

Four are bundled and free: the Berean Standard Bible, the World English Bible, the King James Version, and 개역한글. The BSB is the default because it reads well out loud, which matters more here than in a normal Bible app. ESV and NIV need negotiated licences a solo developer can't get before proving the product works.

iPhone only at launch — no Android, no iPad layout, no web app. If you're on Android there's honestly nothing worth putting you on a list for yet.

When does it launch, and who's building it?

No date — any date given now would be a guess dressed as a commitment. The reader comes first, then the alarm, then the shield, which waits on Apple approving an entitlement. Waitlist members get TestFlight builds in waves and hear about slips as they happen.

One developer, working alone. Not a pastor, not a ministry, not a team. This exists because his own mornings looked exactly like the ones at the top of this page.
